Ground improvement encompasses a suite of geotechnical techniques designed to enhance the engineering properties of soil and rock beneath a proposed structure. In Tacoma, Washington, this category of work is critical because large portions of the city and its industrial waterfront are underlain by loose alluvial sands, silts, soft clays, and uncontrolled fill materials that are prone to settlement, liquefaction, and low bearing capacity. Rather than excavating and replacing these problematic soils—a costly and disruptive process—ground improvement modifies them in place, increasing density, strength, and stiffness to meet project requirements safely and economically.

Tacoma’s geology presents a complex challenge shaped by glacial and post-glacial processes. The city sits on the Puget Lowland, where repeated advances of the Vashon ice sheet deposited layers of glacial till, outwash, and recessional lacustrine sediments. The tideflats and Commencement Bay shoreline are dominated by thick sequences of Holocene alluvium and estuarine deposits, often including liquefiable sands and highly compressible organic silts. These conditions demand rigorous seismic analysis, as the region faces a significant earthquake hazard from the Cascadia Subduction Zone and shallow crustal faults. Ground improvement directly addresses the high liquefaction potential and cyclic softening that standard deep foundations alone cannot always mitigate.

Design and execution of ground improvement in Tacoma must comply with the current edition of the Washington State Building Code, which adopts and amends the International Building Code (IBC). Chapter 18 of the IBC governs soils and foundations, requiring site-specific geotechnical investigations per accepted standards such as ASCE 7 for seismic design. For critical structures, guidelines from the American Society of Civil Engineers (ASCE) and the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) govern the analysis of liquefaction triggering and the selection of remediation methods. Additionally, the City of Tacoma’s Public Works Department enforces its own stormwater and grading ordinances that influence the feasibility and permitting of certain improvement techniques in environmentally sensitive areas.

This category of work supports a diverse range of projects across Tacoma. Warehouse and logistics centers in the Port of Tacoma often rely on ground improvement to support heavy slab loads and mitigate differential settlement on reclaimed tideflats. Mid-rise residential and mixed-use developments in the Dome District and along Pacific Avenue use these techniques to control total and post-seismic settlements in soft soils. Infrastructure projects, including bridge approaches, retaining walls, and roadway widenings, frequently require solutions like stone column design to reinforce embankment foundations and improve drainage. Even smaller commercial builds on sites with undocumented fill benefit from targeted improvement to avoid the expense of deep piling.

Quick answers

What are the primary soil challenges that ground improvement addresses in Tacoma?

The main challenges include liquefiable loose sands and silts in the tideflats and river valleys, soft compressible clays and organic deposits that cause excessive settlement, and undocumented urban fill with variable consistency. Ground improvement mitigates these by densifying granular soils, reinforcing soft cohesive deposits, and creating composite ground with reliable strength and stiffness under both static and seismic loading conditions.

When is ground improvement a better option than deep foundations in Tacoma?

Ground improvement often becomes preferable when a site contains widespread marginal soils that can be treated economically across the entire building footprint. It eliminates the need for structural slabs to span between isolated pile caps, reduces the risk of differential settlement between pile-supported and adjacent slab-on-grade areas, and can simultaneously mitigate liquefaction across a broader zone, which deep foundations alone may not fully address.

How does seismic risk in the Puget Sound region influence ground improvement design?

The region’s high seismicity from the Cascadia Subduction Zone and local crustal faults requires improvement designs to withstand strong ground shaking. Methods are analyzed for liquefaction triggering using site-specific peak ground accelerations per ASCE 7. The design must limit both total and differential post-liquefaction settlement, prevent lateral spreading near slopes or waterfronts, and maintain soil strength to support foundations after the design earthquake event.

What is the typical process for determining the right ground improvement method on a Tacoma site?

The process begins with a comprehensive subsurface exploration including cone penetration tests (CPT) and standard penetration tests (SPT) to characterize soil stratigraphy and engineering properties. A geotechnical engineer then performs settlement and liquefaction analyses under project loads and seismic demands. Based on soil type, depth to competent material, groundwater conditions, and site access constraints, feasible methods are evaluated for technical suitability, constructability, and long-term performance before making a final recommendation.
